Offline Implementation with the NOAA Formula

2024-08-04 — Life Clock Research

One of the design principles of Life Clock is that it works fully offline — no dependency on any API or server. In this article we take a technical look at the NOAA formula for computing sunrise and sunset times, and see how an accuracy of ±2 minutes can be achieved in under 100 lines of code.

Why offline?

Depending on an API for sunrise calculation has several drawbacks:

  • Privacy: every API request sends your geographic location to an external server.
  • Reliability: if the server goes down, the app stops working.
  • Request limits: most free APIs have daily limits.
  • Battery usage: every network request consumes energy.

By using the NOAA formula, all calculations happen locally — no information leaves your device.

The NOAA algorithm

The NOAA Solar Position algorithm consists of three main formulas:

1. Fractional Year

First, the day of year and the hour of day are converted into an angle in radians:

γ = 2π/365 × (dayOfYear − 1 + (hour − 12)/24)

For leap years, the denominator changes to 366.

2. Equation of Time

The Equation of Time gives the difference, in minutes, between true solar time and mean time:

eqTime = 229.18 × (0.000075 + 0.001868·cos(γ) − 0.032077·sin(γ) − 0.014615·cos(2γ) − 0.040849·sin(2γ))

This formula approximates the contribution of Earth's orbital eccentricity and axial tilt. Its value varies between −14 and +16 minutes over the year.

3. Solar Declination

This angle determines the season — i.e., the angle by which the sun is offset from the equator:

decl = 0.006918 − 0.399912·cos(γ) + 0.070257·sin(γ) − 0.006758·cos(2γ) + 0.000907·sin(2γ) − 0.002697·cos(3γ) + 0.001480·sin(3γ)

This angle varies between +23.4° (summer solstice) and −23.4° (winter solstice) over the year.

4. Hour Angle

The hour angle is the angle the sun must traverse to reach the desired event (such as sunrise). For sunrise and sunset, the zenith angle is 90.833° (accounting for atmospheric refraction):

cos(H) = (cos(Z) − sin(lat)·sin(decl)) / (cos(lat)·cos(decl))

If cos(H) > 1, the sun never rises (polar night). If cos(H) < −1, the sun never sets (midnight sun).

5. Local sunrise and sunset times

With the hour angle in hand, sunrise and sunset times in local minutes are computed as follows:

sunrise = 720 − 4·(lon + H·180/π) − eqTime
sunset = 720 − 4·(lon − H·180/π) − eqTime

These values are in minutes from local midnight. To convert to UTC, the longitude (where every 15 degrees equals 1 hour) must be taken into account.

For prayer times (twilight)

For Fajr and Isha, we simply change the zenith angle:

  • Fajr: Z = 108° (sun 18° below the horizon)
  • Isha: Z = 107° (sun 17° below the horizon)

And we use the same cos(H) formula, but with the new angle.

For Dhuhr

Dhuhr is the moment when the sun is at the highest point of its daily arc — i.e., apparent noon. It is computed simply as:

solarNoon = 720 − 4·lon − eqTime

For Asr

Computing Asr is more complex, because it depends on shadow. In the Shia school, it is the moment when the shadow of an object equals its own height (plus the noon shadow). Its formula:

tan(asr_altitude) = 1 + tan(|lat − decl|)

Then the hour angle for this solar altitude is computed:

cos(H_asr) = (sin(−asr_altitude) − sin(lat)·sin(decl)) / (cos(lat)·cos(decl))

Implementation in JavaScript

These formulas have been implemented identically in JavaScript (in the browser) and Kotlin (in the Android app). The JavaScript code is available in the app.js file of this site. The Kotlin code is available in the creativewavecom/lifeclock GitHub repo.

Accuracy and limitations

The NOAA formula performs well at mid-latitudes (such as Iran, 25° to 40° north):

  • Sunrise and sunset: ±2 minutes
  • Dhuhr: ±1 minute
  • Fajr and Isha (twilight): ±5 minutes

The reason twilight times are less accurate is that at low angles, atmospheric refraction is more variable. For higher accuracy, more complex models such as SAEE (Standards of Astronomical Algorithms for Eclipses) can be used, but the computational complexity increases significantly.

For polar regions

In polar regions (north of 66.5° or south of −66.5°), at certain times of the year the sun either never rises or never sets. In this case, cos(H) falls outside the range [−1, 1] and the formula returns NaN. In our app, this case is displayed as "—".

Testing and validation

To ensure the calculations are correct, we compared the formula's output against data from timeanddate.com and aladhan.com for several cities across different seasons. The result: the difference is usually under 2 minutes, which is sufficient for everyday use.

Conclusion

With fewer than 100 lines of JavaScript, you can compute sunrise, sunset, and prayer times accurately enough for everyday use — without needing internet or an external API. This not only protects privacy but also makes the app faster and more reliable.
